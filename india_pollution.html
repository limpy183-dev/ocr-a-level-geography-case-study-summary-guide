Air Pollution & Cancer in India

Epidemiological Transition, PM 2.5, and Health Impacts

1. Context & The Epidemiological Transition
Key Theme: The relationship between economic development, environmental quality, and health (Epidemiological Transition).
  • Current Status: India is currently in Stage 2 of the Epidemiological Transition Model (The Age of Receding Pandemics).
  • The "Double Burden": India faces a unique challenge where it has not fully transitioned to a stage dominated only by Non-Communicable Diseases (NCDs). It battles both infectious diseases (due to sanitation issues) and rising chronic diseases (like cancer and heart disease due to pollution).
2. The Scale of the Crisis (Key Statistics)
99%of India's 1.2bn Breathe Unsafe Air
3+ yearsReduced Life Expectancy (660M people)
Pollution Levels (PM 2.5):
  • WHO safe guideline: <10
  • India's own standard (NAAQS): 10–40
  • Indo-Gangetic plain (incl. New Delhi): Most dangerous category (>90).
  • In winter, Delhi pollution levels can exceed 600.
3. Causes of Pollution
The Main Culprit: PM 2.5
What is it? Particulate matter smaller than 2.5 micrometers (produced by burning fossil fuels).
Mechanism: These particles bypass natural body filters (nose/throat), penetrate deep into the lungs, enter the bloodstream, and interfere with DNA, leading to mutations and cancer.
Sources
Outdoor (Urban/Industrial):
  • Vehicle emissions: Producing Nitrogen Dioxide (NO₂), Sulphur Dioxide (SO₂), and Ozone (O₃).
  • Coal-based power stations and factories.
  • Stubble Burning: Burning crop residue in rural fields contributes up to 17% of India's total emissions. Smoke often blows into cities, worsening smog.
Indoor (Rural):
  • Burning biomass (animal dung, wood, paraffin) for cooking and heating.
  • Impact: Responsible for approximately 1 million premature deaths annually.
4. Impacts on Health
Cancer & Respiratory Disease
+70%Lung Cancer Risk (Delhi)
1 in 5Lung Cancer Cases in Non-Smokers
  • Cancer Risk: Particulate pollution increases the lung cancer risk for Delhi residents by an estimated 70%.
  • Non-Smokers: Historically, smoking was the main cause of lung cancer. Now, 1 in 5 lung cancer cases occurs in non-smokers (a 20% rise in the last decade).
  • Respiratory Symptoms: In Delhi, symptoms like asthma and bronchitis are 1.7× higher than in rural areas.
  • Hypertension: Linked to air pollution, rates are 40% higher in Delhi.
Social & Economic Impacts
  • Inequality: The poor bear the brunt of toxic air because of where they live and work (2011 Delhi Study).
  • Cognitive Decline: Air pollution reduces brain function by several percent, leading to lower educational attainment.
  • Economic Loss: Shorter working lives mean less time to earn, spend, and pay taxes.
5. Management Strategies & Solutions
A. National Strategy: The NCAP
  • Name: National Clean Air Programme.
  • Launched: 2019 (India's first national-level strategy).
  • Goal: Aims for a 20–30% reduction in PM 10 and PM 2.5 concentrations by 2024 (compared to 2017 levels).
Criticism: Targets are not legally binding, so there are no penalties for failure. Progress has been slow.
B. Urban Solutions
  • Transport: 14 Indian cities are expanding high-speed metro systems to reduce car usage.
  • Subsidies: Fuel subsidies (petrol/diesel) have been scrapped in some areas to discourage fossil fuel use.
C. Rural & Industrial Solutions
  • Brick Kilns: Retrofitting to "Zigzag" brick kiln technology reduces coal consumption by around 80%.
  • Stubble Burning: New restrictions and bans implemented (though enforcement is a challenge).
D. Cap-and-Trade Scheme
  • Government sets a legal limit on emissions and allocates permits.
  • Factories emitting less can sell surplus permits to those emitting more.
  • Benefit: Creates a financial incentive to invest in cleaner technology and generates revenue for local authorities to tackle pollution.
6. Challenges to Management
  • Economic vs. Health: For years, the government prioritized rapid economic growth over environmental health, fearing new rules would scare off investment.
  • Enforcement: Civil society activists often have to sue the government for failing to enforce its own laws (Right to Life).
  • Livelihoods: Farmers rely on burning stubble as a cheap/fast way to clear fields; banning it without alternatives harms their income.
