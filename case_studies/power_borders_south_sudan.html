South Sudan

South Sudan

Global governance in a fragile new state

1. Context

South Sudan became independent from Sudan in 2011.

Internal state boundaries matter because political power-sharing has been contested.

South Sudan is the world's newest sovereign state. Independence created legal sovereignty, but political instability and civil war quickly weakened practical state authority.
  • Location: landlocked east-central Africa.
  • Population: 15 million in 2020 in the source material.
  • Economy: government revenue depends heavily on foreign aid, while 96% of exports are crude oil.
  • Development: HDI was 0.413 in 2019, ranked 186th.
  • Poverty: 82% of the population lived below the global poverty line of US$1.90 per day.
2. Why Conflict Developed
  • Since independence, South Sudan has faced political instability, civil war, ethnic rivalry and weak governance.
  • In 2013, political infighting between President Kiir, associated with the Dinka, and Vice-President Machar, associated with the Nuer, escalated into armed conflict.
  • The government struggled to protect citizens, meaning outside intervention became necessary to reduce suffering and support peace.
  • Access to arms remained easy even after the 2018 peace agreement, and UN reports described ethnic cleansing in parts of the country.
Core link: South Sudan shows how a recognised state can still be fragile if the state apparatus cannot provide security, services, rule of law and protection for citizens.

  • Livelihoods were destroyed; much of the population relies on subsistence agriculture.
  • There is acute and persistent food insecurity.
  • Basic services such as health, education, housing and sanitation are limited.
  • Government investment focused on defence and security rather than poverty reduction.
4. Global Governance Response
Organisation Role in South Sudan
OCHA Coordinates UN agencies, NGOs, IGAD and the government; negotiates access, mobilises funding and monitors progress.
UNMISS Protects civilians, monitors human rights and supports delivery of humanitarian assistance; over 16,000 personnel and US$1.27 billion budget in 2019/20.
UNICEF Supports children and mothers despite poor infrastructure; helps release children from armed forces and reunite families.
WFP and WHO Support food security, nutrition, cholera kits, Ebola testing, measles immunisation and anti-malarial treatment.
NGOs Provide shelter, clean water, sanitation, nutrition, education and child protection; examples include MSF, Save the Children and World Vision.
Use South Sudan to show that global governance is multi-scale: the UN, IGAD, government departments, NGOs and local communities must interact for intervention to work.
