Arctic Tundra, Alaska

Water & Carbon Cycles, Oil & Gas Impacts, and Management Strategies

1. Location and Physical Context

20%Earth's Land Surface (Tundra)

  • Location: The North Slope of Alaska lies between the Brooks Range (south) and the Arctic Ocean (north).
  • Key Industrial Site: Prudhoe Bay (Oil and gas discovered here in 1968).
  • Global Context: Tundra environments cover 20% of the Earth's land surface (including N. Russia, Canada, and Alaska).
  • Infrastructure: It is the starting point of the Trans-Alaska Pipeline.
Climate
  • Temperatures: Average annual temp is -28°C. Can drop to -70°C in winter and rise to 12°C in summer.
  • Precipitation: Low; 50 – 350mm per year (mostly snow).
  • Sunlight: Negative heat balance for 8-9 months. In Utqiagvik, the sun does not rise for 65 days in winter.
  • Growing Season: Very short (45 to 90 days).
2. The Water Cycle in the Tundra
Despite low precipitation, the Tundra is often waterlogged in summer because permafrost prevents infiltration and drainage.
Stores
  • Atmosphere: Small store due to low temperature reducing absolute humidity.
  • Groundwater: Limited/None. The Permafrost is a barrier to infiltration, percolation, and recharge.
  • Surface Water: Extensive wetlands, ponds, and lakes form in summer because water cannot drain downwards through the permafrost.
Flows
  • Evaporation: Low rates because most sun energy is used to melt snow, not evaporate water.
  • Transpiration: Limited due to sparse vegetation and short growing season.
  • Run-off: Increases dramatically in summer due to melting snow and the active layer thawing.
Geology/Relief: Low permeability (crystalline rocks) and gently undulating relief (chaotic glacial deposits) impede drainage, contributing to waterlogging.
3. The Carbon Cycle in the Tundra
The Tundra is a massive carbon store but has a very slow flux (flow). Permafrost (soil) is estimated to store 1,600 Gigatonnes of carbon.
Stores
  • Permafrost (Soil): A vast carbon sink estimated to store 1,600 Gigatonnes of carbon.
  • Soil vs Biomass: The amount of carbon in the soil is 5× greater than in the above-ground biomass.
  • Biomass: Small store (4 – 29 tonnes/ha) due to limited water and sunlight.
Flows
  • NPP (Net Primary Productivity): Low, less than 200 grams/m²/year.
  • Decomposition: Slow due to cold temperatures; carbon is locked in the soil rather than released.
  • Flux Seasonality: Carbon flow mainly occurs in summer when the active layer thaws and plants grow rapidly.
4. Human Impacts: The Oil & Gas Industry

Since the 1968 discovery at Prudhoe Bay, the industry has disrupted the local cycles.

A. Impacts on the Water Cycle
Melting Permafrost:
  • Heat Diffusion: Infrastructure releases heat directly to the environment, melting the permafrost.
  • Dust Deposition: Dust from roads settles on snow/ice, darkening the surface (lowering albedo) and increasing sunlight absorption.
  • Vegetation Removal: Construction removes vegetation which naturally acts as insulation for the permafrost.
Consequences of Melting:
  • Melting permafrost and snow cover increases run-off and river discharge.
  • This makes flooding more likely.
  • Increased surface water leads to increased evaporation during summer months.
Disruption of Drainage:
  • Seismic Surveying: Heavy tracks from vehicles create depressions (~15cm deep). These cause "pooling," which alters surface water flow, drains lakes, and further accelerates thawing.
  • Mining: Aggregates (gravel) mining disrupts natural drainage systems.
  • Water Abstraction: Water is abstracted for industrial use and to build ice roads, reducing localised run-off.
B. Impacts on the Carbon Cycle

  • Greenhouse Gas Release: Melting permafrost releases stored gases: CO₂ (7 – 40 million tonnes/year) and Methane CH₄ (24 – 114 thousand tonnes/year).
  • Vegetation Destruction: Industrial activity degrades tundra vegetation, reducing photosynthesis and the uptake of CO₂. Recovery is extremely slow due to the climate.
5. Management Strategies

Strategies aim to moderate the impact on the permafrost (and therefore the cycles).

Strategy How it Works Benefit
Insulated Ice & Gravel Pads Roads/infrastructure built on top of insulating layers Protects permafrost from melting; prevents subsidence
Elevated Buildings & Pipelines Infrastructure built on piles (stilts) to allow cold air to circulate beneath Provides insulation against heat-generating buildings which would otherwise melt the permafrost
Lateral Drilling New tech allows oil/gas to be accessed several km from the drill rig Fewer sites needed, reducing the physical footprint on vegetation and permafrost
Remote Sensing Using satellite/aerial tech to detect reserves Reduces the need for exploration wells, minimizing environmental impact
Refrigerated Supports Used on the Trans-Alaska Pipeline Stabilises the temperature of the permafrost to stop it thawing
6. Key Concepts & Vocabulary
  • Albedo: The reflectivity of a surface (reduced by dust, leading to warming).
  • Permafrost: Permanently frozen ground that stores vast amounts of carbon.
  • Active Layer: The top layer of soil that thaws in summer and freezes in winter.
  • Thermokarst: An irregular land surface formed when ice-rich permafrost thaws.
