Tectonic Hazards

Managing Earthquake Hazards

Comparative Case Study: Japan (AC) vs Nepal (LIDC/EDC)

1. Key Concepts & Definitions
  • AC (Advanced Country): Wealthy, developed financial markets (e.g., Japan).
  • LIDC/EDC (Low Income/Emerging Developing Country): Lower economic development, eligible for poverty reduction support (e.g., Nepal).
  • Modifying the Event: Trying to stop the hazard from happening. NOTE: It is impossible to modify an earthquake event (rapid onset). Strategies focus on modifying vulnerability and loss.
  • Park's Response Curve: A model used to visualize how a country's quality of life changes following a disaster through disruption, recovery, and reconstruction.
2. Japan (Advanced Country)
Context: High economic wealth, stable politics, highly developed education.
A. Modifying Vulnerability (Preparation & Protection)

Japan uses vast resources to increase resilience through technology and planning.

Aseismic Building Design:
  • Cross-bracing: Steel cross braces to stiffen frames.
  • Shear Walls: Concrete walls to reduce rocking.
  • Shock Absorbers: Base isolators made of rubber, steel, or tyres (in lower-income contexts) to absorb horizontal energy.
  • Deep Foundations: Sunk into bedrock to prevent liquefaction (where soft soil behaves like liquid during shaking).
  • Computer-controlled weights: On roofs to counteract movement.
JMA (Japan Meteorological Agency) Warning Systems:
  • Earthquake: World's densest seismometer network allows for text message warnings just before shaking hits.
  • Tsunami: Warning systems in place since 1952.
Community Preparedness:
  • Drills: Occur 4 times a year.
  • Disaster Prevention Day: Held annually on September 1st to practice rescue and emergency responses.
  • Emergency Kits: Homes/workplaces equipped with water, dried food, radios, and torches.
Land Use Zoning: Strict regulations preventing development (e.g., nuclear plants/housing) in high-risk areas or areas prone to liquefaction.
B. Modifying Loss (Response & Recovery)
  • Emergency Services: Well-trained disaster response teams, helicopters, and heavy lifting machinery are deployed immediately.
  • Insurance: Helps individuals and businesses recover economically, though it does not save lives.
  • Reconstruction: Pre-planned recovery strategies at national, regional, and local levels allow for rapid physical and social rebuilding.
  • Evaluation: Following the 1995 Kobe and 2011 Tōhoku quakes, Japan constantly reappraises and updates its infrastructure (e.g., strengthening bridges previously thought safe).
3. Nepal (LIDC / EDC)
Context: 2015 Gorkha Earthquake. Lower financial resources, mountainous topography.
A. Modifying Vulnerability (Preparation)

Despite limited resources, Nepal has made significant efforts, often supported by NGOs.

Organisations:
  • NSET: National Society for Earthquake Technology (Founded 1993).
  • DPNet: Disaster Preparedness Network (Founded 1996).
  • NGOs: Groups like GeoHazards International play a vital role in raising awareness and creating safer schools.
Strategies Implemented:
  • Hazard Mapping: Identifying high-risk "shake zones."
  • Building Codes: Introduced to improve safety, though enforcement can be weaker than in ACs.
  • Education: Annual Earthquake Awareness Day and education programs in schools.
  • Household Preparation: Encouraging "Go-bags" containing dried food, water purification tablets, and whistles.
Success Story: In Bharatpur, risk maps suggested 30% of inhabitants could be killed. Due to planning based on these maps, there were 0 deaths in the city during the 2015 quake, and most infrastructure escaped damage.
B. Modifying Loss (Response)

Nepal relies heavily on international aid due to a lack of internal financial reserves.

International Aid (2015 Event):
  • 330 humanitarian agencies were involved (e.g., UN, EU, Red Cross, Christian Aid, Shelter).
  • India's Contribution: Sent 1,000 Search & Rescue personnel, 13 aircraft, and 10 tonnes of supplies.
  • NGOs: Oxfam provided essential supplies to ensure survival.
Challenges:
  • Topography: Nepal's mountainous terrain hindered attempts to get emergency aid to remote villages.
  • "Cry Wolf" Syndrome: If predictions or warnings happen without an event following, people stop listening to them, failing to evacuate when a real disaster strikes.
4. Summary of Building Design Mitigations

🏗️ High-Tech Solutions (ACs)

  • Automatic window shutters (prevent falling glass)
  • Flexible outer panels
  • Computer-controlled counterweights

🧱 Low-Cost Solutions (EDCs/LIDCs)

  • Tyres filled with stones as shock absorbers
  • Bamboo/lightweight roofs to prevent crushing injuries
Retrofitting: Modifying older buildings to make them safe. Effective but expensive.
5. Conclusion: Inequality in Resilience

🇯🇵 Japan (AC)

High degree of resilience. Resources allow them to bounce back quickly (short-term impacts are managed; long-term recovery is funded).

🇳🇵 Nepal (LIDC/EDC)

Lower resilience. The impacts of earthquake disasters are more serious in the short term and last much longer due to reliance on external aid and difficult geography.
