Bangladesh Floods (2004) & India Air Pollution

Case Study 1: Bangladesh Floods (2004)
Context
  • Location: Bangladesh — a low-lying LIDC in South Asia.
  • Cause: Monsoon flooding exacerbated by deforestation in the Himalayas and climate change.
  • Scale: Two-thirds of the country was underwater.
Health Impacts
800+Deaths
36MPeople Affected
Water-Borne Diseases:
  • Cholera: Contaminated water supplies led to a major outbreak.
  • Dysentery: Spread rapidly through contaminated food and water.
  • Typhoid: Caused by bacteria in sewage-contaminated water.
Other Health Issues:
  • Malnutrition: Crop destruction led to food shortages. Rice production was damaged, causing price spikes.
  • Skin Diseases: From prolonged contact with polluted floodwater.
  • Injuries: From debris carried by floodwaters.
  • Mental Health: Trauma, displacement, and long-term stress.
Responses
  • Short-term: International aid agencies provided clean water, medicine, and food aid. Evacuations and temporary shelters established.
  • Long-term: Investment in flood warning systems, building of raised embankments, improved sanitation infrastructure.
Case Study 2: India Air Pollution
Context
  • Location: India — an EDC experiencing rapid industrialisation.
  • Type: Non-communicable disease burden from environmental hazard (air pollution).
  • Scale: Air quality in major Indian cities regularly exceeds WHO safe limits by 10-20 times.
Causes of Air Pollution
  • Vehicular Emissions: Rapid increase in car ownership and old, inefficient vehicles.
  • Industrial Pollution: Factories, power plants (especially coal-fired), and construction.
  • Agricultural Burning: Crop stubble burning in Punjab and Haryana contributes massively, particularly in autumn/winter.
  • Domestic Fires: Millions of households use biomass (wood, dung, crop residues) for cooking and heating.
Health Impacts

  • Respiratory Diseases: Asthma, chronic bronchitis, and COPD.
  • Cardiovascular Disease: Heart attacks and strokes linked to fine particulate matter (PM2.5).
  • Cancer: Increased risk of lung cancer.
  • Children: Particularly vulnerable. Studies show reduced lung development and increased respiratory infections.
  • Delhi: Often called the "world's most polluted capital." Breathing Delhi air is equivalent to smoking 50 cigarettes a day.
Responses / Mitigation
  • National Clean Air Programme (NCAP): Launched 2019; aims to reduce PM2.5 and PM10 by 20-30% by 2024.
  • Odd-Even Vehicle Scheme (Delhi): Cars with odd/even number plates allowed on alternate days to reduce traffic emissions.
  • Shift to CNG: Delhi mandated all public transport (buses, auto-rickshaws) to switch to Compressed Natural Gas.
  • Smog Towers: Large air filtration structures being trialled in Delhi (effectiveness debated).
Challenges: Enforcement of regulations is weak. Rapid economic growth often prioritized over environmental protection. Many policies are introduced reactively (during pollution spikes) rather than proactively.
