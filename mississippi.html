Coastal Landscapes

The Mississippi River Delta

Low Energy Coastline — Deposition & the Delta Cycle

1. Overview & Location
  • Type: Low Energy Coastline (dominated by deposition).
  • Location: Southern USA, flowing into the Gulf of Mexico.
  • Catchment Area: Covers all or part of 31 US states and 2 Canadian provinces (Alberta and Saskatchewan).
  • River Sections: Divided into Upper, Middle, and Lower. The Lower Mississippi forms the delta environment.
  • Tributaries: Includes the Missouri, Ohio, Arkansas, and Tennessee rivers.

4. Formation & Changes Over Time (The Delta Cycle)
The delta is a Bird's Foot Delta. It is a dynamic landscape formed over the last 7,000 years through a process called the "Delta Cycle."
The Process (Dynamic Equilibrium)
  1. Deposition: Sediment is deposited at the mouth.
  2. Growth: Vegetation grows (Marsh plants/trees), anchoring the coast.
  3. Extension: A delta "lobe" is created over 1,000–2,000 years.
  4. Relocation: As the river path lengthens, it becomes inefficient. The river breaches and finds a shorter, steeper route to the Gulf.
  5. Abandonment: The old lobe is abandoned, sinks, and erodes (often becoming barrier islands).
The 6 Stages of Delta Formation
7,500 – 5,500 years agoSale-Cypremort: Formed when sea levels rapidly rose.
5,500 – 3,500 years agoTeche: Formed after relative sea level rise dropped.
4,000 – 2,000 years agoSt. Bernard: River relocated to the east of New Orleans.
2,500 – 500 years agoLafourche: River relocated to the west of New Orleans.
1,500 years – PresentPlaquemines: The modern "Bird's Foot" delta.
20th CenturyBalize: Emerging delta caused by movement toward the Atchafalaya river (began 500 years ago).
5. Key Coastal Landforms
A. Barrier Islands

Formation: Formed from the reworked sediment of abandoned delta lobes. They protect the habitats behind them from storms.

Example: Chandeleur Islands.

  • A chain of uninhabited islands 50 miles long.
  • Formed over 2,000 years ago as the rim of the ancient St. Bernard Lobe.
  • Change: Shrinking and migrating landward due to hurricane destruction (1915–2005).
B. Coastal Wetlands
  • Significance: 40% of the coastal wetlands in the lower 48 US states are found here.
  • Habitats: Interconnected freshwater, brackish, and salt marshes, ranging from forests to flat grasslands.
  • Formation: Built over thousands of years by floodwaters depositing sediment.
Threats:
  • Human Activity: Construction of levees, canals, and dams (stops sediment deposition/regulates flow) and drainage for urban development.
  • Climate Change: Rising sea levels put these low-lying areas at risk.
C. Beaches
  • Material: Composed of very fine sand (easily eroded).
  • Dynamics: Formed via deposition and smoothed by longshore drift.
  • Characteristics: Changes in coastline direction are frequent, so there are fewer generic features (like tombolos) compared to other coastlines. They are highly vulnerable during storms.
6. Inter-relationships in the Landscape System
  • Linked System: All features (wetlands, islands, delta lobes) are interdependent. If one erodes or builds up, it affects the others.
  • Energy: The system relies on low energy in the lower course to allow deposition.
Current State: The balance has shifted. Sea level rise and hurricane damage have led to a net increase in erosion recently, threatening the delta's dynamic equilibrium.
