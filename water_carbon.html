Water & Carbon Cycles

Global Management of Water & Carbon Cycles

International Agreements, Strategies & Case Studies

Part 1: International Agreements

International cooperation is crucial for managing shared resources like water and carbon.

A. The Paris Agreement (2015)
Goal: Keep the global temperature increase well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels, while pursuing efforts to limit the increase to 1.5°C.
  • Adopted by: 196 countries (known as "Parties").
  • Key Feature: Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) — each country sets its own targets, not binding "from above."
  • Pledges:
    • Developed countries pledge US $100 billion/year to help developing nations transition.
    • Countries must report on actions taken every 5 years.
Limitations/Criticism:
  • 2018 Assessment: Most countries are nowhere near keeping to their NDCs.
  • US Withdrawal: Under President Trump (2017), the US withdrew from the agreement.
  • Non-binding: If a country doesn't meet its commitment, there are no penalties or consequences.
B. UN Water Convention (1997)

Aimed to establish uniform global rules for the use and management of international rivers and lakes (shared by more than one country).

  • Core Principle: Equitable and reasonable use of shared waters.
  • Entered into force: 2014 (after 35 nations had ratified it).
Limitations: Key countries in water-scarce regions (e.g., Ethiopia, Turkey) have NOT signed, rendering it somewhat ineffective. Nations sharing major rivers like the Nile and Euphrates opted out.
Part 2: Carbon Management Strategies
A. Carbon Capture & Storage (CCS)
The process of capturing CO₂ emissions from sources like cement factories and power plants, compressing it, and transporting it to a storage location (usually deep underground geological formations).
Example: The Sleipner Project (Norway):
  • Located in the North Sea.
  • The world's first commercial CCS project (operational since 1996).
  • Stores approximately 1 million tonnes of CO₂ per year under the seabed.
  • Equivalent to removes 215,000 cars from the road annually.
CCS Criticisms: Very expensive; doesn't address the root cause (fossil fuel dependence); risk of leaks from underground storage; limited proven sites.
B. Carbon Tax
A direct tax levied on the carbon content of fuels or emissions. Designed to make businesses and consumers pay for their pollution.
  • Sweden: Introduced in 1991. Has a rate of €114/tonne (one of the highest in the world).
  • Impact: Swedish emissions fell by 26% since 1991, while GDP grew by 78%, proving decoupling of growth and emissions is possible.
C. Emissions Trading (Cap and Trade)
  • Governments set a "cap" on total emissions. Companies receive or buy carbon credits.
  • Companies emitting less can sell their spare credits to those who need more.
  • EU ETS: Largest scheme; covers ~10,000 installations (45% of EU emissions).
Criticism: Critics argue it can be easily gamed and doesn't actually reduce emissions fast enough. Carbon prices can crash if too many credits are issued.
Part 3: Water Management Strategies
A. Sustainable Drainage Systems (SuDS)
SuDS aim to manage surface storm-water by mimicking natural processes.
  • Examples: Green roofs, permeable paving, rain gardens, bioswales (planted channels).
  • Benefits: Reduce flood risk, improve water quality, create green spaces, recharge groundwater.
B. Desalination
Converting seawater into freshwater.
  • Method: Reverse Osmosis — forces seawater through membranes to remove salt.
  • Scale: Saudi Arabia is the world's largest producer; the Jubail plant produces 1 billion litres per day.
Drawbacks: Extremely expensive and energy-intensive. Produces toxic brine waste that harms marine ecosystems.
C. Rainwater Harvesting
A simple, low-tech solution to water scarcity, particularly suitable for arid/semi-arid EDCs.
  • Method: Collecting and storing rainwater from rooftops or land surfaces.
  • Example: Rajasthan, India: Village-level "johads" (check dams) built to capture monsoon rain, recharging local wells and providing water during dry seasons.
Part 4: Threatened Landscape — The Arctic Tundra
The Arctic Tundra is a critical environment for both the water and carbon cycles. Climate change is profoundly altering its systems.
Carbon Stores
Permafrost (permanently frozen ground) stores an estimated 1,672 gigatonnes of organic carbon — nearly twice the amount of carbon currently in the atmosphere.
Impact of Climate Change
  • Melting Permafrost: Thawing releases stored carbon as CO₂ and methane into the atmosphere, creating a positive feedback loop (more warming → more melting → more carbon release → more warming).
  • Albedo Effect: Melting ice and snow reduces reflectivity (albedo), so the Earth absorbs more solar energy, further accelerating warming.
  • Sea Level Rise: Melting ice sheets contribute to rising global sea levels, threatening low-lying coastal areas worldwide.
  • Ecosystem Change: Shrubs and trees are growing in areas previously too cold (known as "Arctic Greening"), altering habitats.
Water Cycle Changes
  • Thermokarst Lakes: Form as permafrost melts and the ground subsides, creating new water bodies.
  • Increased River Flow: Melting ice feeds rivers, increasing discharge and potentially flooding downstream areas.
  • Impact on Natural Systems: Changing water availability disrupts migration patterns (e.g., caribou) and vegetation growth.
