Tectonic Hazards

Mount Merapi, Indonesia

EDC — Volcanic Hazard & the 2010 Eruption

1. Context and Tectonic Setting
  • Location: Indonesia is a vast archipelago (collection of islands) stretching 3,000 km. Mount Merapi is located on the island of Java.
  • Tectonic Plate Boundary: A Destructive (Convergent) plate margin located at a subduction zone on the Pacific Ring of Fire.
  • Process: The Indo-Australian Plate (oceanic) is subducting beneath the Eurasian Plate (continental).
  • Volcano Type: Strato-volcano (Composite). It reaches 3,000m in altitude and is the most active volcano in Indonesia, erupting regularly since 1548.
Population Pressure: Java is densely populated. Over 11,000 people live on the slopes of the mountain; over 40,000 live in the wider hazardous area.
2. Why do people live there?

Despite the risks, people choose to live near Merapi for several reasons:

  • Fertile Soil: Weathered volcanic lava creates rich soil, allowing for 3 crop cycles per year. Most residents are subsistence farmers.
  • Mining: Sulphur mining on the mountain sides provides income.
  • Geothermal Energy: Being developed to provide work and power.
  • Cultural/Spiritual Connection: Locals have a strong bond to their village sites and family fields.
3. The 2010 Eruption
Characteristics: High explosive eruption featuring pyroclastic flows, lava bombs, ash clouds, poisonous gases, and lahars.
Primary Impacts (The Event)
  • Pyroclastic Flows: Extended 4km to the North, 11.5km to the West, 7km to the East, and 15km to the South.
  • Ash/Material: 130 million m³ of material was deposited.
  • Lava Bombs: Thrown 4km from the summit in all directions.
  • Sulphur Emissions: High levels caused acid rain over the region.
Social Impacts
  • Death Toll: 367 people were killed (35 specifically in Kinahrejo; 33 in the Amat River Valley due to lahars).
  • Injuries: 277 people injured (burns, respiratory problems, broken bones).
  • Displacement:
    • 410,000 people in the local area became refugees.
    • 100,000 evacuated to crowded emergency shelters.
    • 75,000 residents packed into government camps long-term.
  • Housing: Villages were incinerated; 2,682 people eventually had to be moved to permanent safer homes.
  • Cultural: Many ignored scientific warnings because a spiritual "Gatekeeper" told them to stay and protect the land.
Economic Impacts
  • Total Loss: Economic losses estimated to surpass $600 million.
  • Aviation: Yogyakarta airport closed for over 2 weeks. Flights cancelled as far away as Jakarta and Western Australia due to ash.
  • Agriculture: Large numbers of livestock killed. Ash and pyroclastic flows destroyed crops. Vegetable prices soared due to shortages.
  • Infrastructure: Power lines, bridges, and buildings destroyed.
Environmental Impacts
  • Forestry: 8,600 acres (more than half) of the national park was damaged by ash and lava.
  • Recovery: Estimated 5 years for the forest to return to its previous state.
  • Lahars: Heavy rainfall (>1800mm per annum) mixed with ash to create lahars (mudflows), inundating river valleys (e.g., Magelang and Jumoyo).
4. Management and Mitigation
Preparedness and Monitoring (Vulnerability Reduction)
  • Agency: The CVGHM (Centre for Volcanology & Geological Mitigation), established in 1920, monitors the volcano using seismographs, satellite observation, and gas readings.
  • Zoning: Permanent settlement is forbidden on the highest slopes.
  • Relocation Incentives: Villagers on southern slopes (highest risk) encouraged to relocate with financial assistance and a small plot of land.
Short-Term Response (Emergency)
  • Evacuation: 210 temporary centres set up in schools and stadiums.
  • Rescue: 1,600 volunteers and military personnel deployed.
  • Aid:
    • National Disaster Mitigation Agency donated $2.6 million.
    • WHO distributed 6 tons of biscuits and blankets.
    • 100,000 face masks provided.
  • Compensation: Government offered 3,200 compensation for a smaller plot of land elsewhere. An 11 million program was launched to buy cows to keep farmers off the slopes.
Long-Term Response (Recovery)
  • Rehousing: REKOMPAK housing project set up. 16 new sites selected for rebuilding villages. Government provided 30 million Rupiah per household for rebuilding.
  • Lessons Learned: Need for larger evacuation zones, permanent shelters, and better public education to counter spiritual beliefs that ignore scientific warnings.
Challenges
  • Resource Limitation: As an EDC, Indonesia has limited funds/resources to deploy during a crisis compared to developed nations (like Japan).
  • Human Factors: High population density and spiritual beliefs make evacuation difficult.
  • Climate: The humid tropical climate and rainy season (March–November) creates a persistent secondary hazard of lahars.
