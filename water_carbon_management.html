Water & Carbon Cycles

Global Management of Water & Carbon

Forestry, Allocations, Drainage Basin Planning & Emissions Reduction

Part 1: Global Management of the Water Cycle
1. Forestry & Protection of Tropical Forests
Forests are crucial for the global water cycle (via transpiration and interception) and the carbon cycle (sequestration). Protecting them is essential for maintaining both cycles.
Global Initiatives:
  • UN REDD Programme: Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Forest Degradation.
  • World Bank FCPF: Forest Carbon Partnership Facility — funds over 50 partner countries across Africa, Asia-Pacific, and South America.
Case Study: The Amazon (Brazil) — ARPA
  • Programme: Amazon Regional Protected Areas (ARPA).
  • Partners: UN, World Bank, WWF, German Development Bank.
  • Covers nearly 10% of the Amazon Basin (strictly protected).
  • Impact: Stabilises the regional water cycle and offsets 430 million tonnes of carbon a year.
  • Methods: Financial incentives, carbon offsets, and direct funding to support indigenous forest communities and ecotourism.
2. Water Allocations
The Problem (Agriculture):
  • Agriculture is the biggest consumer globally.
  • It accounts for 70% of water withdrawals.
  • It accounts for 90% of water consumption.
  • Wastage: Occurs through evaporation and seepage (inefficient management like over-irrigation).
Management Techniques:
  • Minimising evaporation: Mulching, zero soil disturbance, drip irrigation.
  • Reducing run-off: Terracing, contour ploughing, vegetative strips.
  • Water Harvesting: Storage in ponds/reservoirs.
Regional Examples:
  • Lower Indus Valley (Pakistan): Water agreements divide resources between downstream states. The Punjab and Sindh regions receive 92% of the Indus's flow.
  • Colorado Basin (USA): Resources are allocated to California, Arizona, Nevada, Utah, and New Mexico. The vast bulk is used for irrigation.
3. Drainage Basin Planning
A. International Scale: The Nile Basin (Hydropolitics)
  • Length: World's longest river (6,500 km).
  • Catchment: 2.9 km² (covers 10% of Africa).
  • Dependence: Runs through 10 countries with 360 million people depending on it for survival.
The Conflict (Upstream vs. Downstream):
  • Source: About 85% of the water originates from Eritrea and Ethiopia (Upstream).
  • Usage: 94% of the water is used by Sudan and Egypt (Downstream).
  • Tension: High evaporation rates due to climate change and historical treaties (1929/1959) that gave Egypt dominance. Ethiopia now wants to use the river for HEP plants and industrial development.
Management Strategies:
  • Nile Basin Initiative (NBI): A system of cooperative management started in the late 1990s. All countries (except Eritrea) work with the World Bank.
  • The Helsinki Rules (1996): International guidelines regulating how transboundary rivers and groundwater are managed to avoid "Water Wars."
  • Tech Fix: 'Tecconile' — a joint GIS system to help monitor and plan the basin.
B. National Scale: United Kingdom (England & Wales)
  • Concept: Management is most effective at the drainage basin scale (holistic approach).
  • EU Water Framework Directive: Under this directive, 11 River Basin Districts have been defined in the UK (e.g., Severn, Thames, Humber).
  • Management Plan: Published jointly by the Environment Agency and Defra.
Specific Targets:
  • Water quality improvement.
  • Abstraction rates.
  • Groundwater levels.
  • Flood control and floodplain development.
  • Status of habitats and wildlife.
Techniques:
  • Surface Water: Conserving/restoring wetlands.
  • Groundwater: Limiting abstraction and using artificial recharge (injecting water into aquifers through boreholes).
Part 2: Global Management of the Carbon Cycle
1. Wetland Restoration
Wetlands occupy 6–9% of Earth's surface but contain 35% of the terrestrial carbon pool.
The Issue: In the lower 48 US states, wetland area has halved since 1600. Destruction releases stored CO₂ and CH₄.
Case Study: Canada's Prairies
  • Lost 70% of wetlands.
  • Restoration target: 112,000 ha.
  • Sequestration potential: 364,000 tonnes of Carbon/year.
Case Study: UK
  • Target to restore 500 ha by 2020.
  • Example: 400 ha of grade 1 farmland in East Cambridgeshire being converted back to wetland.
Pro: Long-term stable carbon sink (more secure than forests).
Con: Newly restored wetlands can emit Methane (CH₄) — a gas 23× more potent than CO₂ — in the early years.
2. Afforestation
Planting trees where there were none to act as carbon sinks. A mature forest can store 100–200 tonnes of carbon per hectare.
Case Study: China
  • Massive project (started 1978) aiming to afforest 400,000 km² by 2050 (roughly the size of Spain).
  • Between 2000–2009, 30,000 km² were successfully planted.
Pro: Combats desertification and land degradation.
Con: Slow impact (takes 20–50 years to become significant); monocultures (e.g., eucalyptus) can reduce biodiversity and deplete soil water.
3. Agricultural Practices
The Issue: Intensive livestock farming produces 100 million tonnes/year of CH₄.
Strategies:
  • Zero Tillage: Growing crops without ploughing to conserve soil organic content.
  • Polyculture: Growing crops interspersed with trees to protect soils from erosion.
  • Livestock Management: Improving animal feed to reduce enteric fermentation (methane).
  • Rice Strains: Introducing drier-condition rice varieties to reduce methane from flooded paddies.
4. International Agreements (Reducing Emissions)
  • Kyoto Protocol (1997): First legally binding agreement. Failed because major polluters (USA, China, India) were exempt or did not ratify.
  • Paris Agreement (2015): 196 countries agreed to keep global warming below 1.5°C – 2°C. Total pledges of $1.3 trillion per year.
5. Cap and Trade
A market-based approach where businesses are allocated an annual quota for CO₂ emissions.
Case Study: EU Emissions Trading Scheme (ETS)
  • Largest scheme worldwide, covering 12,000 installations and 45% of EU emissions.
  • Success: A target of 21% reduction by 2020 was reached 6 years early.
Regional Successes:
  • Ontario, Canada: Sold permits raising over $504 million for community projects.
  • California, USA: Emissions have dropped annually since introduction.
Evaluation: Effectiveness of Strategies
Water Cycle
  • Forestry: Effective at stabilizing regional cycles (e.g., Amazon), but relies on international funding and political will.
  • Allocations: Crucial for immediate survival in arid regions (Indus/Colorado), but agricultural demand (90% consumption) is difficult to reduce significantly.
  • Drainage Basin Planning: Highly effective in the UK (Advanced Country) due to clear governance (Defra/Environment Agency). In the Nile (LIDC/EDC context), it is harder to implement due to geopolitical tensions, though the NBI shows progress.
Carbon Cycle
  • Wetland Restoration & Afforestation: Critical for sequestering carbon and reversing long-term atmospheric changes.
  • Cap and Trade & Afforestation: Show measurable success, but International Agreements are often hindered by political changes (e.g., changes in US leadership) and economic interests of developing nations (China/India).
  • Interdependence: Strategies often overlap (e.g., Forestry protects both cycles). Successful management requires international cooperation and recognition of intergenerational equity.
