North Yorkshire — High-Energy Rocky Coastline

Location: North East England, facing the North Sea.

1. Physical Factors Influencing the Coast
A. Wave Energy (High Energy Environment)
  • Fetch: The dominant waves come from the North and Northwest, with a fetch of over 1500 km starting from the Arctic.
  • Wave Height: Monitoring at Whitby Bay (2010-11) showed wave heights often exceeding 4 m, even in summer.
  • Erosion: High wave energy means erosion dominates over deposition. The most exposed north-facing areas (e.g., Saltburn) receive the highest energy inputs.
  • Longshore Drift: Strong drive from North to South.
B. Geology (Lithology and Structure)
  • North York Moors: Rise up to 400 m above sea level. Composed mainly of Jurassic sandstones, shales, and limestones.
  • Flamborough Head: A large chalk headland topped with glacial till (deposit from the Devensian Glacial Period).
  • Bedding: Rocks are horizontally bedded, creating vertical cliff faces.
Erosion Rates (Crucial Data):
Weak Shale and Clay: ~0.8 m per year
Resistant Sandstone and Limestone: <0.1 m per year
C. Sediment Sources
Sediment Cell: Sub-cell 1d (Part of major cell 1: St Abbs to Flamborough).
  • Nearshore: Driven onshore as sea levels rose at the end of the last glacial period.
  • Cliff Erosion: Sandstone, chalk, and boulder clay deposits (yields significant gravel).
  • Rivers: The River Esk is the only large river, but supplies limited sediment due to the construction of weirs and reinforced banks.
Sediment Budget Data: Net increase in beach sediment of 9245 m³ between 2008–2011 at Saltburn.
2. Landforms and Inter-relationships
A. Headlands and Bays (Discordant Coastline)

Formed due to varied rock resistance (sandstones/limestones vs. shales/clays).

  • Robin Hood's Bay: Eroded into weak shales.
  • Headlands: More resistant sandstone forms Ravenscar (south) and Ness Point (north).
  • Filey Bay: Developed in weak Kimmeridge Clay, flanked by resistant limestone and chalk.
B. Cliffs
  • Structure: Horizontally bedded sedimentary rocks create vertical profiles.
  • Flamborough Head: Vertical chalk cliffs 20–30 m high.
  • Glacial Till: Overlying the cliffs, lowered by mass movement to an angle of about 40°.
  • Stepped Profiles: Found between Robin Hood's Bay and Saltburn, reflecting varied geology (steep sandstone slopes vs. gentler clay slopes).
C. Shore Platforms

Example: Robin Hood's Bay (eroded into Lower Lias shales).

  • Typical angle: 1° (ramped sections up to 15°).
  • Maximum width: ~500 m (extends further offshore).
  • Formation: Likely formed within the last 6000 years (stable sea levels), though some experts argue they are relict features from inter-glacial periods.
D. Headland Features (Caves, Arches, Stacks, Geos)
  • Wave Refraction: Concentrates energy on resistant headlands.
  • Selwick's Bay (Flamborough): Master joints in chalk are enlarged to form caves and arches.
  • Green Stacks Pinnacle: An example of an isolated stack following the collapse of an arch roof.
  • Geos: Over 50 geos formed along this coastline, mostly aligned NE or NNE (facing dominant waves).
  • Blowholes: Formed where vertical master joints expand; clay collapses into underlying caves causing funnel-shaped depressions on cliff tops.
E. Beaches
  • State: Few well-developed beaches due to high energy removing sediment.
  • Location: Found in sheltered low-energy environments (Scarborough, Filey Bay).
  • Lack of Spits: Despite considerable longshore drift, spits are absent due to:
    • High tidal range (~4 m).
    • Lack of estuarine environments to provide sediment sinks.
3. Changes Over Time

The landscape system changes on vastly different timescales:

Timescale Examples of Change
Seconds Rapid changes such as major cliff collapses or the collapse of an arch/blowhole (e.g., creating a Geo).
Seasonal Changes in beach profiles (storm beaches in winter vs. berms in summer).
Millennia Formation of major headlands and bays. Shore platform formation (last 6000 years). Glacial deposition (100,000 years ago).
